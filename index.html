Why I made this

When a dozen things happen on the roads at once, which one needs the operator now, and can they trust what they see?

In South Australia, operators went from 11 separate systems to one alarm list and map, so all of it lands in front of one person (Transmax case study).

I haven't worked in transport, so before applying for a design role in this space I wanted to understand that moment better.

Not STREAMS, and not connected to Transport and Main Roads or Transmax.

About this concept · 2 of 4

Three guesses

  1. 1
    Unplanned firstCrashes, hazards, floods and congestion before roadworks. Then priority, lanes blocked, time.
  2. 2
    Flag old informationNo update for over an hour? The screen says so before anyone acts on it.
  3. 3
    The list leads, the map follows (least sure)Pick from the list and the map moves to it.

Also: priority never relies on colour alone, everything works from the keyboard, and it's dark for dim control rooms. All to check with operators.

About this concept · 3 of 4

What I'd ask

When you rebuild a tool operators know by muscle memory, how do you change it without slowing them down?

  • In the first minute of a motorway crash, what do you look at first?
  • How do you know someone else is already on it?
  • Which alerts do you ignore, and why?

I've never seen a real control room. These are the questions that would tell me which guesses are wrong.

How I made it

  1. ReadTransmax's case studies and the QLDTraffic data spec
  2. Decidedthe problem and the three guesses
  3. Built and testedwith Claude Code, on keyboard, phone and laptop

Changed after testing

  • Markers didn't load: the map reported ready too early
  • The dark map hid the roads, so main roads are brighter
  • On phones, details covered everything; now a half-height sheet
  • On laptops, the map was squeezed; details now float over it

The closest real work I've done to this: designing every failure state of a regulated share trade at Liquidise.
